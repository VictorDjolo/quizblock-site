How to stop doomscrolling: what actually helps

Feeds are built to have no stopping point, and scrolling runs on habit. Here is why it is so hard to put the phone down, and the practical changes that make it easier.

You pick up your phone to check one message and surface half an hour later, a little flatter and not sure where the time went. That's doomscrolling: carrying on through a feed long after it stopped being useful or enjoyable. The word was coined for bingeing on bad news, but the pattern is the same with any endless feed. If you want to stop doomscrolling, or any kind of mindless scrolling, the encouraging part is that it isn't mainly about willpower. It comes down to how feeds are built and how habits work, and you can work with both.

Why feeds are so hard to put down

Rewards you can't predict

Since B. F. Skinner's experiments on reinforcement in the mid-twentieth century, psychologists have known that rewards which arrive unpredictably keep behaviour going more stubbornly than rewards on a fixed schedule. A feed works in a similar way. Most posts are fine, some are dull, and every so often one is funny, outrageous or exactly what you were hoping for. You can't tell which swipe will bring the good one, so every swipe feels worth a try.

No natural place to stop

A chapter ends. An episode ends, even if the next one starts on its own. A feed just keeps loading. With no built-in stopping point, ending is something you have to decide, and you have to decide it at the moment you're least inclined to: tired, bored and mid-swipe.

It runs on autopilot

Most scrolling isn't chosen. It's a habit, set off by a cue: a spare minute, a dull task, getting into bed, the first moment at your desk. Habits are triggered by situations rather than decisions, which is why "I'll just use it less" rarely lasts. By the time you notice, you're already scrolling.

How to stop doomscrolling: change the setup, not your character

Because the pull comes from the design and the habit, the most reliable fixes change your surroundings and settle decisions ahead of time, instead of relying on resisting in the moment.

1. Add friction

Small obstacles have an outsized effect on habits. Habit researchers, Wendy Wood among them, call this friction: the more effort a behaviour takes, the less often it happens on autopilot, even when the extra effort is tiny. A few seconds is often enough for the deliberate part of you to catch up with the automatic part.

  • Log out of the sites you scroll most, so each visit starts with a password.
  • Remove them from your bookmarks bar and new-tab shortcuts.
  • Move the apps off your phone's home screen, or into a folder on the last page.
  • Charge your phone outside the bedroom.

2. Decide in advance with if-then plans

The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer calls them implementation intentions: plans that link a specific situation to a specific response, in the form "if this happens, then I'll do that". He set out the idea in a 1999 paper, and a 2006 review of many studies, written with Paschal Sheeran, found that people who make plans like these follow through on their goals more often than people who only set the goal.

The trick is to be concrete:

  • "If I sit down at my desk, then I open my task list before anything else."
  • "If I catch myself scrolling in bed, then I put the phone on the charger across the room."
  • "If I want to check Instagram, then I'll do it after lunch, for ten minutes."

You make the decision once, calmly, instead of a dozen times while the feed is pulling at you.

3. Replace the habit instead of just removing it

The cue doesn't go away when the feed does. You'll still get bored, still want a break, still reach for something between tasks. If nothing else is ready, you'll drift back. So keep a few replacements to hand, short enough to fit the same few minutes: a walk round the block, a glass of water, a stretch, a few pages of a book, a message to a friend.

4. Make your own stopping points

If the feed won't end, give it an ending. Decide how long before you open it, set a timer, or tie it to something with a natural finish, like a cup of tea. Stopping on purpose, even after a long session, feels very different from being dragged away.

5. Be patient with slips

You'll have evenings where it all goes out of the window. That's normal. In a well-known 2010 study of how people form new habits, Phillippa Lally and colleagues found that missing a single day didn't set people back in any meaningful way. Pick up again the next day rather than deciding the whole thing has failed.

Notice how you feel after a long scroll, not during it. A quick mental note ("tired, a bit flat") is often more persuasive next time than any rule.

A plan for this week

  1. Pick your one or two worst feeds. Not every app, just the ones that cost you most.
  2. Add friction today. Log out, clear the shortcuts, move the apps, or put a gate in front of them.
  3. Write two if-then plans. One for when you most often start scrolling, one for when you most often can't stop.
  4. Choose three replacements. Things you can do in five minutes, wherever you usually scroll.
  5. Look back after seven days. Keep what helped, drop what didn't, and adjust.

None of this means feeds are off limits. The aim is to use them when you've decided to, not because the next swipe happened to be there. If one kind of feed is the main problem, such as short videos, you can also block just YouTube Shorts and keep the rest.
