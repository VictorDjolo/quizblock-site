How to learn every country in the world: a practical plan

The UN has 193 member states, and you can learn them all a few at a time. A region-by-region plan, tactics for the hardest parts of the map, and a way to make it stick.

Learning every country in the world sounds like a big job. The United Nations has 193 member states, and plenty of them are small, oddly shaped or easy to confuse. But it breaks down well. Take it one region at a time, practise for a few minutes a day, and space out your reviews, and you can learn all the countries and memorise the world map without ever sitting down to a long study session. Here's a plan.

Which countries count?

Lists of "all the countries" differ, so pick one. The simplest is the UN's 193 member states. Two more, the Holy See and the State of Palestine, are UN observer states, and some places with disputed or limited recognition, such as Kosovo and Taiwan, aren't UN members. Learn the 193 first. The rest are easy to add once the map around them is familiar.

For grouping, the UN Statistics Division uses a standard set of regions known as M49. It has a few surprises: Russia counts as Europe, while Türkiye, Cyprus and the countries of the Caucasus count as Asia. Grouped by continent, with Central America and the Caribbean counted as part of North America, the 193 split like this:

How to learn all the countries, region by region

Start where it's easiest, so you build a frame of familiar shapes before you tackle the crowded parts.

  1. South America (12). Few countries, with big, distinct shapes: a good first win. Take care with Guyana and Suriname on the north coast. French Guiana, next to them, is part of France rather than a country of its own.
  2. North America (23). Canada, the United States and Mexico are easy. Central America's seven countries run in a chain from Guatemala and Belize down to Panama. The Caribbean takes longer: learn the big islands first (Cuba, Jamaica, and Hispaniola, shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic), then the small island nations to the east.
  3. Europe (43). Many people know the west already. Spend your time on the Balkans and the band of countries from the Baltic to the Black Sea, and don't forget the small ones: Andorra, Liechtenstein, Monaco, San Marino and Malta.
  4. Asia (47). Split it up: the Middle East, Central Asia, South Asia, East Asia and Southeast Asia. Central Asia and the Gulf states are where most of the effort goes.
  5. Africa (54). The biggest group, and the one where most people have the most to learn. Go sub-region by sub-region (north, west, central, east and southern) rather than all at once.
  6. Oceania (14). Australia, New Zealand and Papua New Guinea are easy. The Pacific island nations are small and far apart, so learn them in their island groups: Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia.

The hardest parts of the map, and how to crack them

Some parts of the map trip up nearly everyone. A few tactics help.

Similar names

Guinea, Guinea-Bissau and Equatorial Guinea are all on Africa's Atlantic coast, while Papua New Guinea is on the other side of the world. The Republic of the Congo and the much larger Democratic Republic of the Congo are neighbours. Niger sits north of Nigeria. Dominica is a small island in the eastern Caribbean, while the Dominican Republic shares Hispaniola with Haiti. And Slovakia and Slovenia are two different countries that don't even share a border.

Learn confusable names together, side by side, so you learn the difference between them rather than two vague shapes.

Small countries

Microstates and island nations are hard to see, let alone remember. Use anchors: Lesotho sits entirely inside South Africa. The Gambia is a thin strip along its river, surrounded by Senegal except at the coast. San Marino is surrounded by Italy, and Liechtenstein sits between Switzerland and Austria.

Crowded patches

The Balkans, Central Asia, the Gulf and West Africa pack a lot of countries into small spaces. Learn each country with its neighbours, as a cluster, rather than in alphabetical order. Odd facts help a cluster stick: Uzbekistan, for example, is one of only two doubly landlocked countries in the world (the other is Liechtenstein), so reaching the ocean means crossing at least two borders.

Practise both ways: from the map and to the map

Finding a country when you're given its name isn't the same as naming a country when you're shown it. Practise both: "Where is Malawi?" and "Which country is this?". Each direction makes you retrieve the country by a different route, and between them they cover how you'll actually meet it, in the news, on a trip or in a book.

Once a region is going well, add connected facts: capitals, flags, which countries share a border. Each new connection gives you another path back to the same country.

Memorise the world map little and often

The fastest way to forget a map is to learn it all in one evening. Memory research is consistent here: short sessions where you test yourself, spread out over days, beat long sessions of staring at a map. The guide to spaced repetition explains why.

  • Test, don't look. Cover the names and try first. Checking after you've committed to an answer is what makes it stick.
  • Bring back what you miss. A country you got wrong today should come back later today and again tomorrow. One you got right can wait a few days, then a week, then longer.
  • Add a few new countries a day. At five new countries a day, you'd meet all 193 in under six weeks, with reviews keeping the earlier ones fresh.
  • Mix regions once you're going. Switching between regions feels harder than drilling one, but it helps you tell similar places apart.

Keep a short list of the countries that keep slipping. Look them up on a map with their neighbours, then test yourself on them first in your next session.
